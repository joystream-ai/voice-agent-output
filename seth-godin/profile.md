# Seth Godin: voice study

> An analysis of Seth Godin's published writing: what the writing does and how often, with evidence. It is not a skill that rewrites drafts.

Built from 11 posts on seths.blog dated November 10-19, 2026 (S1-S11, newest first). All are blog posts, so every pattern below is a blog pattern.

## Identity and tone

Seth Godin is a marketer and author who has written a short daily blog for about twenty years. In these posts he states a position in plain declarative sentences, hedges the claim ("almost", "might", "probably") instead of shouting it, and speaks to the reader as "you" while sharing the failing as "we". Posts are short: a claim, one small example, a turn, and a closing line that hands the reader the consequence. Mean sentence length is 14.0 words (median 13) across 140 sentences, and 39 of 82 paragraphs are a single sentence.

## Recurring convictions

- **R1** Easy-to-measure signals and neat theories are suspect: ask what the thing is for, or reconsider the theory, before collecting more data. Sometimes, 3 of 11 (S1, S5, S9); E4, E5. Use only when the draft already argues this.
- **R2** Selling and marketing mean going to where the other person already is, not asking them to come to you or to do you a favor. Sometimes, 3 of 11 (S3, S4, S9); E9, E10. Use only when the draft already argues this.
- **R3** Lasting results come from a persistent process, not from a single launch or push. Sometimes, 2 of 11 (S2, S7); E13. Use only when the draft already argues this.

## Structural patterns

- **R4** Open with a declarative sentence that states the claim or the situation; never open with a question. Always, 11 of 11 pieces (for example S2, S5, S9, S10); E3.
- **R5** End with a short closing paragraph that states the takeaway as a plain claim, a definition or a reframe, and cut any recap or closing question. Usually, 9 of 11 pieces (S1, S2, S3, S4, S5, S6, S7, S9, S10); the other 2 are promotions that end on an offer or a link (S8, S11). In 3 of the 9 (S1, S3, S5) the final line is a conditional that begins If or And if, at most once per piece; E4, E5, E9, E13.
- **R6** End with the title's key phrase repeated in the last line. Sometimes, 2 of 11 (S9, S10), at most once per piece; E7.
- **R7** Replace an abstract claim with one small named example (a food, a team, a crowd) before generalising. Usually, 6 of 11 pieces (S1, S3, S5, S7, S9, S10); E6.
- **R8** Move two parallel points into a numbered list after a colon sentence that names how many there are. Sometimes, 2 of 11 (S7, S10), at most once per piece; E8.

## Sentence mechanics

- **R9** Split any paragraph that runs past three full sentences. Usually, 8 of 11 pieces (S1, S3, S4, S5, S6, S7, S8, S11) keep every paragraph to three sentences or fewer (76 of 82 paragraphs overall); the exceptions are S2, S9 and S10.
- **R10** Move the turn of the argument into its own one-sentence paragraph that opens with And, But, Except or Of course. Usually, 7 of 11 pieces (S1, S2, S3, S4, S5, S9, S10); E2, E10.
- **R11** Replace a flat absolute claim with a hedged one ("almost", "might", "probably" or "perhaps"). Usually, 9 of 11 pieces (S1, S2, S3, S4, S5, S6, S7, S9, S10); the promotion posts S8 and S11 have none; E4, E5.
- **R12** Cut the comma before and or or in a three-item series. Always, 5 of 5 pieces that contain such a series (S2, S4, S5, S6, S9), and none of the 11 has a serial comma; E1, E5.
- **R13** Move a qualifying aside into parentheses inside the sentence instead of starting a new sentence. Sometimes, 5 of 11 (S1, S4, S6, S7, S9), at most twice per piece; E3.
- **R14** Never add an em dash. Always, 11 of 11 pieces have none; the only dashes are unspaced en dashes in S6 and S10; E12.

## Vocabulary

- **R15** Open with "Of course," the sentence that concedes the obvious before the turn. Sometimes, 4 of 11 (S1, S3, S4, S5), at most once per piece; E2.
- **R16** Keep "you" for the reader's choice and "we" or "our" for the shared habit, and use both in one piece. Usually, 6 of 11 pieces (S4, S5, S6, S7, S8, S9); E10, E11.

## Per-platform notes

- Blog: observed (11 of 11 pieces, seths.blog).
- LinkedIn, X, Instagram, email: silent. Ask before writing for these platforms.

## What this voice is not

- **R17** Cut subheadings and bold section titles inside a post. Always, 11 of 11 pieces have none; a capitalised label opens a paragraph in 2 of them (S3, S8), which is not a heading.
- **R18** Never add a plug, a link to buy or a call to act inside an argument post. Always, 9 of 9 argument posts (S1, S2, S3, S4, S5, S6, S7, S9, S10); the 2 promotion posts (S8, S11) hold the offers.
- Author's preference: none given.

## Confidence notes

- Strong: R4, R5, R11, R12, R14, R17, R18 (counted over 9 to 11 pieces each).
- Thin: R1, R2, R3, R6, R8 rest on 2 or 3 pieces. R13, R15 and R16 are in 4 to 6 pieces.
- Built from 11 posts, all published within ten days of November 2026, so seasonal topics (a holiday book bundle) and the current subject (AI) are over-represented. Topic words were not made rules for that reason.
- Single-piece observations, not rules: the two-word fragment pair in S2 ("Foops aren’t swell. Foops fail."), the march story with its 399,996 rejections in S9, and the italic one-line question in S1 and S9 ("_What’s it for?_", "_It was rejected._"; 2 of 11 but each is a different device).
- S8 and S11 are promotions and carry little argument voice; the rules mostly describe the 9 argument posts.
- 18 rules passed the six tests and are kept. More posts from other years would firm up R1 to R3 and R6 to R8.
